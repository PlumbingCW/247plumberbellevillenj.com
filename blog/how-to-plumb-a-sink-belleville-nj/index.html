How to Plumb a Sink: A Belleville, NJ Plumber's Guide

.

Plumbing a sink means connecting hot and cold supply lines to the faucet and connecting the drain through a P-trap to the wall. The steps are simple on a swap and harder on a new location, where drain slope, venting and code come into play.

This guide walks through a like-for-like replacement and points out where a licensed plumber should take over. For urgent leaks, our line is open 24/7 at (973) 488-5410.

Know what you are changing

There are two kinds of sink jobs. A replacement keeps the existing supply and drain locations. A new sink in a new spot needs new pipes, a vent and often a permit.

This guide covers a replacement. If you are moving a sink or adding one, the work changes from a repair to an installation, and the rules are stricter. See our plumbing installation page for that.

Tools and parts to gather first

Having everything ready avoids a mid-job trip to the store.

  • Adjustable wrench and basin wrench for tight faucet nuts.
  • Slip-joint pliers and a bucket for the trap.
  • Plumber's putty or silicone, as the sink manufacturer directs.
  • New braided supply lines in the right length.
  • A new P-trap kit with washers if the old one is worn.
  • Pipe thread tape for threaded connections.
  • Towels and a flashlight.

Take a photo of the existing setup before you start so you can match it later.

Step by step: replacing a sink

Take your time and keep the water off until the end.

  1. Shut off the waterClose the two small valves under the sink. Open the faucet to drain the lines.
  2. Disconnect the drainPlace a bucket under the trap and loosen the slip nuts. Remove the trap and tailpiece.
  3. Remove the old sink or faucetDisconnect supply lines, release the mounting nuts and lift the unit out.
  4. Set the new sinkClean the opening, apply sealant as directed and set the sink or faucet in place.
  5. Connect the supply linesAttach new braided lines to the faucet and the shutoff valves, hand tight plus a small turn.
  6. Connect the drainInstall the strainer, then the tailpiece and trap, lined up without strain.
  7. TestOpen the valves slowly, run water, and check every joint with a dry paper towel.

The P-trap and why it matters

The P-trap is the curved pipe under the sink. It holds a small plug of water that blocks sewer gas from rising into the house. It also catches small items that fall down the drain.

If the trap is installed too low, too high or without a proper vent, it can siphon dry and let odors in. That is one reason new sink locations need a plumber who knows venting rules.

Slope and alignment

The drain must slope slightly toward the wall so water and waste flow away. Pipes that run uphill or sag collect debris and clog.

Line up the trap and tailpiece before tightening. A trap that is forced into place will leak over time. Plastic fittings are forgiving, but they still need straight alignment.

When a sink job is not a DIY job

Call a licensed plumber if the shutoff valves will not close, the supply lines are corroded or galvanized, the drain is cast iron or lead, or you need to move the sink. Those jobs often reveal larger problems behind the wall.

In New Jersey, plumbing work must be done by a licensed master plumber or their employees, with limited exceptions for owners working on their own homes. Ask the Belleville township office at 973-450-3300 what applies to your project.

Choosing the right supply lines

Braided stainless supply lines are flexible and easy to install. Pick the right length so there is a gentle curve and no kink. Avoid old rigid chrome tubes, which crack at the nut. Check the size of the shutoff outlet and the faucet inlet, since both vary.

Mistakes that cause callbacks

Overtightening plastic nuts cracks them and causes slow leaks. Skipping thread tape on threaded connections leaves weeping joints. Reusing an old compression washer on a new trap invites drips. Forgetting to run the water for a full minute before you declare success lets a slow leak go unnoticed.

Safety steps before you start

Close the shutoff valves, open the faucet to relieve pressure and switch off any disposal at the breaker before you reach into the cabinet. New Jersey's plumbing subcode adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code Illustrated, 2021 edition, through N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15. It governs trap arm length, slope and venting for the drain you are connecting to.

Quick reference: what changes professional sink work

If you hire a plumber, these factors move the quote.

  • Replacement or relocationMoving a sink costs far more than swapping one.
  • Shutoff valve conditionSeized valves are replaced first.
  • Pipe materialOld galvanized or cast iron needs special handling.
  • Cabinet accessA cramped vanity takes longer.
  • TimingAn evening call costs more than a weekday one.

Insurance note: a sudden leak from a failed supply line may be covered. A slow leak that has rotted the cabinet may not be. Photograph any damage first.

Belleville note: older homes and old shutoffs

Many Belleville houses date from the early and mid twentieth century. Under-sink shutoff valves in those homes are often original and seize when turned. If a valve will not close, do not force it. Close the main and call.

Belleville's water is soft by the 2023 water quality report, listed at 48.7 ppm hardness, so scale is less of a problem on faucets here than in harder-water towns. Old valves still fail from age and mineral crust.
